The first year of MDEF moves quickly. You enter unfamiliar contexts, work with different people and technologies, make things, test them and move on. It gives you a broad field in which to discover how you work, what you care about and what kinds of questions keep returning.

By the end of that year, something begins to take shape: your **hybrid profile**. Year 2 starts from there.

The question is no longer only **how do you approach something you do not yet understand?** It becomes: **what do you want to do with the way of working you have developed?**

This year gives you time to stay with a question for longer, go deeper into a particular context and decide where you want your work to operate. This might lead towards research, a studio practice, a cultural project, a technological platform, an organisation, a business, a scientific investigation or something deliberately between several of these.

The point is not to choose a professional label. It is to begin constructing **your own space of practice**.

### From multiple sprints to one sustained project

Year 1 is structured around short cycles. Year 2 concentrates that experience into a much longer one. At the centre of the year is the **Thesis Project**: an individually developed project that grows across all three terms.

This change in rhythm matters. A sprint allows you to enter a situation, formulate a position, experiment and learn quickly. A year-long project asks you to decide what deserves to remain open, revisit assumptions and develop methods and relationships over time. Prototypes can become interventions, interventions can produce evidence, and evidence can change the direction of the project.

You are no longer responding primarily to a brief defined by the programme. **You are defining the territory yourself.**

This does not mean beginning from a blank page. Your project will sit within existing histories, disciplines, infrastructures, communities and bodies of knowledge. The challenge is to understand where you are entering, what is already there and what your contribution might be.

A thesis might investigate a neighbourhood, an infrastructure, a technology, a community, a material process or a cultural practice. It might result in an object, interface, protocol, platform, service, performance, organisation or strategy. What matters is developing a proposition that is **specific enough to act upon, open enough to learn from and grounded enough to be accountable to its context**.

### A first-person project

Year 2 develops what we call a **first-person perspective**. This does not mean that the project is about you. It means recognising that you are part of the situation you are researching and designing within. Your choices, skills, access, assumptions and relationships affect what you can see and what you can do.

There is no neutral position from which to design. Who are you working with? Why this context? What gives you the legitimacy to intervene? What forms of knowledge are you relying on? What are you unable to see? What are you supporting, resisting or attempting to reframe?

The project becomes a way of answering these questions **through practice rather than through statements alone**.

### Situate → Explore + Build → Reflect

The structure should already feel familiar. Year 2 returns to **Situate, Explore, Build and Reflect**, but stretches this cycle across the academic year. Instead of repeating it six times, you stay with one evolving project long enough for the phases to become deeper and less clearly separated.

They are not isolated stages. An intervention may force you to rethink the context, while reflection may reveal the need for another experiment. The project moves backwards as well as forwards.

### Term 1: Situate

The first term is about establishing where the project stands. You identify the context in which you want to work and research what is already happening there: the people, practices, technologies, institutions, theories and forms of knowledge that shape it.

You also look for weak signals, not as abstract predictions about the future, but as changes already becoming visible in the present. Artificial intelligence, housing, biotechnology or climate change are still much too large as project territories. Where does the issue become tangible? Who encounters it? Through which infrastructures, technologies, regulations or everyday practices? Where might your own practice meaningfully enter?

Situating a project means reducing abstraction without reducing complexity.

From this work, you develop a **research question** and a **hypothesis**. The aim is not simply to find an interesting topic, but to establish a position: what does the project support, resist or reframe, and where does it sit in relation to existing practices?

By the end of the term, the project should have a clearer context, question and direction, without pretending that the answer is already known.

### Term 2: Explore + Build

The second term shifts from defining the project towards developing it through action. You might prototype, organise, fabricate, code, interview, perform, map, publish, convene people or intervene directly in an existing situation.

The methods depend on the project. What matters is that they are not decorative additions to the research. **The method is part of the project itself.**

This is where research-through-design becomes particularly important. You do not need to understand everything before you act. An intervention can itself produce knowledge, but it also creates responsibilities.

**Something happens because you were there.**

A behaviour changes, a conversation starts, a device produces data, a relationship forms, an institution reacts, a prototype fails or an assumption turns out to be wrong. These responses become evidence when you can explain what they changed in your understanding of the project.

Through interventions, experiments and case studies, you progressively develop the project proposition: the artefact, system, protocol, service, organisation, platform, strategy or other form that the project has become.

### Term 3: Reflect

The final term asks you to step back without stepping away. You formalise the project and examine what it has produced. What knowledge has emerged? What does it contribute? What might others use, adapt, question or continue? Where are its limits?

Reflection is not simply an evaluation at the end of the process. **It is another design act.**

You decide how the project should be communicated, to whom and through which forms. You also consider **continuity**. For some projects this may involve partnerships, funding, distribution or a sustainable business model. For others it may mean further research, an open-source community, a publication, a cultural programme or a collective practice.

Scaling does not necessarily mean making something bigger. It can mean making a method transferable, strengthening a local infrastructure, allowing knowledge to circulate or creating the conditions for others to continue the work.

The question is not simply **how can this grow?** It is **what should continue, under what conditions, and with whom?**

The year concludes with the public defence of the project.

### Research, technology and specialised knowledge

The Thesis Project is the centre of Year 2, but it does not stand alone. Courses and electives provide methods, technologies and specialised knowledge that you can bring into your own work.

**Research & Methods** supports the development of an independent research process, from research questions and state-of-the-art analysis to methodologies, experiments and the organisation of the thesis.

**Emergent Technologies** extends the technical capacities developed during the first year. You select modules from the Fab Academy programme according to the needs of your practice, including areas such as embedded programming, electronics, networking, moulding and casting or machine design.

Other courses introduce perspectives on applied research, innovation, economies and the organisational conditions through which projects operate. The intention is not to create disconnected assignments. Where possible, these courses should become **resources for the thesis**.

This requires becoming selective. You do not need to use every method, technology or skill encountered during Year 1. Some will become central and others will remain in the background. The question is **what the project actually requires**.

### Mentoring, collaborators and external expertise

As projects become more specific, the expertise they require will diverge. From the second term onwards, individual external mentors complement the core Thesis Project faculty and are selected according to the particular territory of each project.

The programme can provide a common framework, but it cannot contain every field you might decide to enter. Part of developing an independent practice is learning how to build the network of knowledge around it: finding collaborators, identifying who knows something you do not and bringing different forms of expertise into conversation.

In this way, the project begins to move beyond MDEF and into the communities of practice it wants to participate in.

### Documentation as research

During the first year, documentation records what happened across many short projects. During the second, it becomes the memory of a much longer process.

It should make visible how the project changed: which questions disappeared, which experiments failed, which encounters redirected the work and which decisions became important. A photograph, diagram, field note, prototype, fragment of code or discarded test can become evidence of how an idea developed.

Do not reconstruct a perfectly linear story at the end. **Document the uncertainty while it is happening.**

By the end of the year, the thesis should communicate not only **what you made**, but also **how you came to understand why it should exist**.

### What the second year is really about

Year 1 gives you six opportunities to try different versions of yourself. Year 2 asks you to decide which questions are worth staying with.

You bring with you the technical abilities, research methods, references, collaborations and forms of making accumulated through the sprints. But the purpose is not to demonstrate everything you have learned. **It is to become more selective.**

Your project may become more scientific, political, entrepreneurial, artistic, spatial, technological or cultural. What matters is that these decisions begin to form a coherent practice, with its own questions, methods, relationships and forms of evidence.

The Thesis Project is a first substantial attempt to define **where you want to operate, what you want to contribute and how you intend to work**.
Year 1 helped you construct a hybrid profile.

**Year 2 asks you to put it into the world.**
