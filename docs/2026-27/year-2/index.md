The first year of MDEF moves quickly. Across six sprints, you enter unfamiliar contexts, work with different people and technologies, make things, test them and move on. The repetition is intentional. It gives you a broad field in which to discover how you work, what you care about and what kinds of questions keep returning.

By the end of that year, something begins to take shape: your **hybrid profile**. Year 2 starts from there.

The question is no longer only **how do you approach something you do not yet understand?** It becomes: **what do you want to do with the way of working you have developed?**

This year gives you time to stay with a question for longer, go deeper into a particular context and decide what kinds of knowledge and practices you want to engage with. For some projects, this might lead towards research or an academic trajectory. Others may develop into a studio practice, a cultural project, a technological platform, a new organisation, a business, a scientific investigation or something that sits deliberately between several of these.

The point is not to choose a professional label. It is to begin constructing **your own space of practice**.

### From six sprints to one sustained project

Year 1 is structured around many short cycles. Year 2 concentrates that experience into a much longer one. At the centre of the year is the **Thesis Project**: an individually developed project that grows across all three terms.

This change in rhythm matters. A sprint allows you to enter a situation, formulate a position, experiment and learn quickly. A year-long project asks something different. You need to decide what deserves to remain open, revisit assumptions and develop methods more deliberately. Relationships with communities, collaborators and contexts can develop over time. Prototypes can become interventions, interventions can produce evidence, and evidence can change the direction of the project.

You are no longer responding primarily to a brief defined by the programme. **You are defining the territory yourself.**

This does not mean working alone or beginning from a blank page. Your project will inevitably sit within existing histories, disciplines, infrastructures, struggles, communities and bodies of knowledge. The challenge is to understand where you are entering, what is already there and what your contribution might be.

The scale and form of the project can vary considerably. A thesis might investigate a neighbourhood, an infrastructure, a technology, a community, a material process, a cultural practice or a planetary system. It might result in an object, interface, protocol, platform, service, performance, organisation, strategy or a combination of these.

What matters is developing a proposition that is **specific enough to act upon, open enough to learn from and grounded enough to be accountable to the context in which it operates**.

### A first-person project

Year 2 develops what we call a **first-person perspective**. This does not mean that the project is about you. It means recognising that you are part of the situation you are researching and designing within. Your choices, skills, access, assumptions and relationships affect what you can see and what you can do.

There is no neutral position from which to design. The task is therefore to make your position visible and accountable. Who are you working with? Why this context? Why this technology? What gives you the legitimacy to intervene? What forms of knowledge are you relying on? What are you unable to see? What are you supporting, resisting or attempting to reframe?

The project becomes a way of answering these questions **through practice rather than through statements alone**.

### Situate → Explore + Build → Reflect

The structure should already feel familiar. Year 2 returns to **Situate, Explore, Build and Reflect**, but stretches this cycle across the entire academic year. Instead of repeating it six times, you stay with one evolving project long enough for these phases to become deeper and less clearly separated.

They are not isolated stages. Questions established while situating the project may return during prototyping. An intervention may force you to rethink the context. Reflection may reveal the need for another experiment. The project moves backwards as well as forwards.

### Term 1: Situate

The first term is about establishing where the project stands. You identify the specific context in which you want to work: a place, community, technological system, environmental condition, cultural practice, institution, network or combination of these. You research what is already happening there and identify practitioners, theories, projects and forms of knowledge that help you understand the field.

You also look for weak signals, not as abstract predictions about the future, but as changes already becoming visible in the present. Artificial intelligence, food systems, housing, biotechnology or climate change are still much too large as project territories. Where does the issue become tangible? Who encounters it? Through which infrastructures, institutions, technologies, regulations or everyday practices? What has already been tried, and where might your own practice meaningfully enter?

Situating a project means reducing abstraction without reducing complexity.

From this work, you develop a **research question** and a **hypothesis**. The aim is not simply to find an interesting topic, but to establish a position. What does the project actively do? What does it take a stand on? What does it support, resist or reframe? Where does it sit in relation to existing practices?

By the end of the term, the project should have a clearer context, question and direction, without pretending that the answer is already known.

### Term 2: Explore + Build

The second term shifts from defining the project towards developing it through action. Research becomes increasingly entangled with practice. You might prototype, organise, fabricate, code, interview, perform, map, publish, convene people, build infrastructures or intervene directly in an existing situation.

The particular methods depend on the project. What matters is that they are not decorative additions to the research. **The method is part of the project itself.**

This is where the research-through-design approach developed during Year 1 becomes particularly important. You do not need to understand everything before you act. An intervention can itself become a way of producing knowledge. But interventions also create responsibilities.

**Something happens because you were there.**

A behaviour changes, a conversation starts, a device produces data, a relationship forms, an institution reacts, a prototype fails, a community refuses something or an assumption turns out to be wrong. These responses become evidence. A prototype can be evidence. An intervention can be evidence. A failed experiment can be evidence.

Evidence becomes useful when you can explain what it changed in your understanding of the project. By working through interventions, experiments and case studies, you progressively develop the project proposition: the artefact, system, protocol, service, organisation, platform, strategy or other form that the project has actually become.

### Term 3: Reflect

The final term asks you to step back without stepping away. You formalise the project and examine what it has produced. What knowledge has emerged through the work? What does it contribute to its context? What might other people use, adapt, question or continue? Where are its limits?

Reflection here is not simply an evaluation at the end of the process. **It is another design act.**

You decide how the project should be communicated and to whom, and what forms of documentation allow others to understand not only the result but the process and evidence that produced it. You also consider **continuity**: what might happen to the project after the academic year ends.

For some projects this may involve partnerships, funding, distribution or a sustainable business model. For others it may mean an open-source community, further research, a cultural programme, a publication, a collective practice or another structure capable of carrying the work forward.

Scaling does not necessarily mean making something bigger. A neighbourhood intervention does not automatically need to become a global platform. Scaling can mean making a method transferable, allowing an idea to travel, strengthening a local infrastructure, publishing knowledge openly, building a community around a practice or creating the conditions for other people to continue the work.

The question is not simply **how can this grow?** It is **what should continue, under what conditions, and with whom?**

The year concludes with the public defence of the project.

### Research, technology and specialised knowledge

The Thesis Project is the centre of Year 2, but it does not stand alone. A series of courses and electives surround it, providing methods, technologies and specialised knowledge that can be brought into your own work.

**Research & Methods** supports the construction of an independent research process: locating and evaluating sources, developing research questions, positioning work within a state of the art, choosing methodologies, designing experiments and organising the thesis itself.

**Emergent Technologies** extends the technical capacities developed during the first year. Rather than following a single technical curriculum, you select modules from the Fab Academy programme according to the needs of your developing practice. These can include embedded programming, electronics design and production, networking, moulding and casting, machine design and other fabrication processes.

Other courses bring perspectives on applied research, innovation, economies and the organisational conditions through which projects operate. The intention is not to create several disconnected projects. Where possible, these courses should become **resources for the thesis**.

If you need to design electronics, learn electronics. If the project requires a distributed network, study networking. If your research depends on a new fabrication process, develop it. The curriculum becomes increasingly something you navigate in relation to the practice you are trying to construct.

This also requires becoming more selective. You do not need to use every method, technology or skill you encountered during Year 1. Some will become central and others will disappear into the background. The question is no longer how much you can add to your practice, but **what the project actually requires**.

### Mentoring, collaborators and external expertise

As projects become more specific, the expertise they require will also diverge. From the second term onwards, individual external mentors complement the core Thesis Project faculty. Mentors are selected according to the particular territory of each project and can come from very different fields. You may also identify practitioners or researchers whose work is particularly relevant to what you are developing.

This is another important shift from Year 1. The programme can provide a common framework, but it cannot contain every field you might decide to enter. Part of developing an independent practice is therefore learning how to build the network of knowledge around it: identifying who knows something you do not, finding collaborators, asking better questions and bringing different forms of expertise into conversation.

Your project begins to move beyond the educational environment of MDEF and into the communities of practice it wants to participate in. The people you meet may become interviewees, critics, collaborators, partners or future colleagues. Learning how to locate and enter these communities is itself part of the project.

### Documentation as research

During the first year, documentation allows you to record what happened across many short projects. During the second year, it takes on another role: it becomes the memory of a much longer process.

Documentation should make visible how the project changed, which questions disappeared, which experiments failed, which encounters redirected the work and which decisions became important. A photograph, diagram, field note, prototype, fragment of code, conversation or discarded test can later become important evidence of how an idea developed.

Do not reconstruct a perfectly linear story at the end. Research rarely happens like that.

Document the uncertainty while it is happening.

By the end of the year, the thesis should therefore communicate not only **what you made**, but also **how you came to understand why it should exist**.

### What the second year is really about

Year 1 gives you six opportunities to try different versions of yourself. Year 2 asks you to decide which questions are worth staying with.

You bring with you the technical abilities, research methods, references, collaborations and forms of making accumulated through the sprints. But the purpose is not simply to demonstrate everything you have learned. **It is to become more selective.**

You may move closer to technology or further away from it. Your project may become more scientific, political, entrepreneurial, artistic, spatial, cultural or infrastructural. What matters is that these decisions begin to form a coherent practice, with its own questions, methods, technical capacities, relationships and forms of evidence.

This practice might enter into conversation with research, industry, culture, technology, public institutions or communities without necessarily belonging entirely to any one of them. The Thesis Project is a first substantial attempt to define **where you want to operate, what you want to contribute and how you intend to work**.

Year 1 helped you construct a hybrid profile.

**Year 2 asks you to put it into the world.**
