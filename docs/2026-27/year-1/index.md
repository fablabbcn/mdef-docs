The **Master in Design for Emergent Futures** began in 2017. This is its ninth edition. Almost a decade and more than 200 alumni, the programme has accumulated methods, experiments, mistakes, projects, communities and ways of teaching that have gradually changed what MDEF is. The structure you are entering is an attempt to bring that experience together.

MDEF starts from a fairly simple proposition: **the future is already unevenly distributed through the present**. Climate breakdown, artificial intelligence, migration, extractive infrastructures, new forms of labour, biotechnology, platform economies, changing bodies and changing ideas of nature are not abstract scenarios waiting somewhere ahead of us. They are already reorganising everyday life.

So we are not particularly interested in predicting the future.

We are interested in learning how to **notice what is emerging, understand the systems producing it, take a position, and intervene**.

This is why MDEF has traditionally described its practice as working with alternative presents rather than speculative futures. It also means moving away from design understood simply as the production of objects or solutions. The programme works across technologies, territories, cultures and forms of collective life, asking what kinds of relationships design participates in producing.

You will read, talk and argue. But you will also walk, observe, code, solder, fabricate, interview, map, sew, grow, perform, dismantle things and build things that do not necessarily work the first time.
The point is not to separate critical thinking from making. **Making is one of the ways we think**.


### The first year: six sprints

MDEF 01 is organised around six intensive sprints, two per term:

1. **Homes–Borders–Tourist**
2. **Monsters–Enchantments–Pleasures**
3. **Pets–Criatures–Others**
4. **Rabbit Holes–Infrastructures–Labour**
5. **Time–Natures–Compost**
6. **Bodies–Fun–Machines of Belief**

These titles are deliberately unstable. They are not six disciplinary subjects to master. They are collections of tensions: ways of entering larger questions through things that are close enough to observe, inhabit and manipulate.

A home can become a question about ownership, displacement, care or tourism. A party can become a way of studying bodies, technologies, desire and social norms. A pet might open questions about domestication and multispecies relations. A data centre might reveal otherwise invisible geographies of computation and labour.

The first sprint, for example, begins in the **Parc Agrari and Delta del Llobregat**, where agriculture, airport infrastructures, tourism, ecological protection, urbanisation and multispecies habitats overlap. Rather than treating the territory as an empty site for design, students investigate who is already there, what infrastructures sustain it and who has the power to move, occupy, extract, protect or belong.

The second moves into another territory: the body and the party. Wearable technologies, DIY sensors and collective performance become ways of investigating how technologies participate in producing ideas of normality, pleasure, desire and monstrosity.

### Situate → Explore → Build → Reflect

The new structure grows out of the four tracks that have shaped the program over previous editions: **Situate, Explore, Build and Reflect**. 
Rather than running alongside one another as separate courses, they now form a repeated cycle inside every sprint.

**Situate** means going somewhere.

You begin in a real context, encountering the people, infrastructures, species, conflicts and histories already operating there. You work from first-person experience without pretending that your own position is neutral. You observe before assuming you understand. You learn to turn a broad problem into a situated question.

**Explore** means opening possibilities before deciding what the answer is.

Local practitioners, collectives, researchers and makers join the programme to introduce technologies, techniques and practices through experimentation. Emerging technologies sit next to obsolete ones, industrial processes next to vernacular knowledge, digital tools next to bodies and materials. The aim is technological literacy without technological solutionism.

**Build** means committing ideas to matter.

You prototype at 1:1 whenever possible. You fabricate, programme, assemble, test, fail and iterate. A prototype is not simply the representation of an idea that already exists in your head. It is an instrument for discovering what you did not yet know. The programme explicitly expects observations from one iteration to alter the next.

**Reflect** means putting the work back into the situation that produced it.

You test what happens when an artefact meets bodies, institutions, environments, infrastructures or publics. The prototype becomes evidence. What did it make visible? What relationships did it produce? What resisted your assumptions? What failed? What would you change?

Then the cycle begins again.

Across the year you will repeat this rhythm six times, moving through very different contexts, technologies and scales. The intention is not that you become an expert in six topics. It is that the repetition gradually changes **how you approach something you do not yet understand**.

### Beginning and ending each term

Each term starts with a **Kick Off**: an intensive moment to get moving again, build trust, work together and disturb some of the habits we inevitably bring with us.

Two sprints follow.

Each term then closes with a **Wrap Up**. This is a different kind of space. You step away from the immediate demands of collective production and look across what you have done. Through individual mentoring, feedback and review, you begin to identify connections between your interests, abilities, positions and ways of working.

This matters because the program is not trying to produce one recognisable kind of designer.

The first year is about constructing what we call your **hybrid profile**: not a predetermined professional identity, but a particular combination of practices, questions, technical capacities and positions that becomes increasingly yours. The programme structure explicitly returns to one-to-one Hybrid Profile reviews at the end of each term.

### Declarations of Commitments

Every sprint also begins with: a **Declaration of Commitments**.

As a team, you formulate a hypothesis. You decide how you intend to investigate it. You commit to gathering evidence through both more conventional research methods and research-through-design: observation and interviews might sit alongside experiments, prototypes and iterative making. Then you sign it.

This is not supposed to freeze the project.

You are allowed to change your mind. In fact, sometimes you should.

But if the hypothesis changes, if an experiment fails, or if the direction of the project shifts, the change should become visible and explainable. The Declaration becomes a living record of what you thought you were going to do, what actually happened, and what you learned in between.

Documentation therefore matters. A failed prototype can be evidence. A disagreement can be evidence. A photograph showing why you abandoned an idea can be evidence. The commitment document is progressively built across **Situate, Explore, Build and Reflect**, rather than reconstructed retrospectively at the end.

It is a small mechanism for making research accountable, but also for taking uncertainty seriously.

### What the year is really about

By the end of MDEF 01 you will have made many things. Some will survive. Some probably should not.

The more important outcome is the practice that begins to form between them.

It is this practice that you will carry with you: a growing set of skills, methods and ways of working, but also a position from which to act. Across six sprints, you will also build a substantial body of projects and documentation that can become part of your portfolio. Together, these will shape the questions you ask, the collaborations you build, the work you choose to pursue and the futures you choose to participate in.
