    Glossary

    A shared vocabulary

    This glossary brings together the concepts, methods and critical perspectives that inform MDEF, alongside the terminology that defines how the programme is organised. It distinguishes between a collaborative lexicon, open to interpretation, discussion and contributions from students and faculty, and a more structured reference to the programme's learning formats, processes and expectations. Together, they establish a shared language while recognising that knowledge, practices and their meanings continue to evolve.

    Programme Terminology⇝

    Term:

    One of the three main teaching periods of MDEF. Each term follows a shared rhythm: a kickoff, two four-week sprints and a wrap-up that connects the work to each student's developing hybrid profile.

    Track:

    A continuous area of learning and practice running across the programme. MDEF's four tracks are Situate, Explore, Build and Reflect. In the MDEF 01 2026–27 structure, each track takes the form of a module within every sprint, connecting its methods across different contexts.

    Wrap-up:

    The closing review of each term, connecting experiences across sprints through individual mentoring, feedback and reflection. Students revisit their Declarations of Commitments from both sprints to assess their research, processes and outcomes, identify relationships between their questions, skills and positions, and consider the direction of their developing hybrid profile and practice.

    Kickoff:

    The opening period of each MDEF term, establishing a shared starting point through orientation, collective activities or research. The first kickoff introduces the programme, its communities, facilities and foundational fabrication practices.

    Sprint:

    An intensive four-week cycle of situated research, experimentation, prototyping and reflection organised around a set of thematic tensions. MDEF 01 includes six sprints, two per term, each containing the Situate, Explore, Build and Reflect modules and documented through a Declaration of Commitments.

    Module:

    A defined teaching unit within a sprint, with its own activities, faculty and assessment criteria. In MDEF 01, each sprint contains four modules, Situate, Explore, Build and Reflect, which enact the programme's four tracks in a specific context.

    Situate:

    The MDEF track concerned with grounding an inquiry in a real context. Within each sprint, the Situate module uses fieldwork, first-person experience and research to identify existing actors, histories and tensions, establish a position and formulate a question and hypothesis.

    Explore:

    The MDEF track concerned with opening possibilities through experimentation with technologies, materials and practices. Within each sprint, the Explore module works with practitioners and collectives to develop techniques and findings that can inform Build, including emergent and alternative technologies.

    Build:

    The MDEF track concerned with developing ideas through making. Within each sprint, the Build module translates explorations into functional prototypes through fabrication, programming, assembly, testing and iteration, working at full scale wherever possible.

    Reflect:

    The MDEF track concerned with critically examining what a design process and intervention produce. Within each sprint, the Reflect module returns prototypes to their context through deployment, use or performance, then interprets feedback, consequences and failures through critique and documentation.

    Collaborative Lexicon⇝

    Agency:

    The capacity to act and affect a situation. In MDEF, agency is considered relational: what a person, collective, organism or technology can do depends on its connections, material conditions and relations of power.

    Alternative present:

    A different way of living, making or relating that can be enacted and tested in the present. Alternative presents challenge conditions treated as inevitable, opening possibilities beyond business as usual through situated interventions and lived experience.

    Alternative technologies:

    Technologies and techniques that offer other possibilities than dominant industrial or commercial approaches. These may include vernacular, analogue, obsolete, repaired or repurposed systems. Their value is examined through their context, accessibility, dependencies and consequences, rather than their novelty alone.

    Autobiographical design:

    An approach in which designers use their own sustained experience of designing and using a system as material for design research. Personal experience can reveal insights, but its limits and relevance to others must also be considered. Neustaedter, C. and Sengers, P. (2012). Autobiographical design: what you can learn from designing for yourself. Interactions, 19(6), 28–33.

    Autoethnography:

    A qualitative research approach that describes and systematically analyses personal experience to understand its cultural and social context. It connects an individual account to wider relations, practices and structures.

    Bill of materials:

    A record of the materials and components required to construct a prototype, including quantities and relevant specifications. In MDEF, it forms part of the documentation that allows others to understand, reproduce or adapt the work.

    Borders:

    Conditions that separate, filter, regulate or connect territories, bodies and ways of living. Borders may operate materially, politically, socially, ecologically or symbolically, shaping who and what can move, remain, belong or access resources.

    Boundaries:

    The limits through which a situation, community or field of inquiry is defined. Asking who is included in a collective “we”, who is excluded and how these limits are negotiated helps reveal which possibilities a design process can open or close.

    Co-shaping:

    The reciprocal process through which technologies shape human practices and relations, while those practices and relations shape technologies. Design participates in this process rather than acting on an independent technical object. See Verbeek, P.-P. (2006), Materializing Morality.

    Collaborative practice:

    Working with others to develop and share questions, knowledge, tools, skills and responsibilities. Collaboration includes making contributions and disagreements visible, recognising existing practices and returning findings to those involved.

    Communities of practice:

    Groups whose members develop knowledge through ongoing participation in a shared practice. For MDEF students, engaging with such communities means learning from existing work, building relationships and contributing beyond the immediate requirements of a project.

    Contextual feedback:

    Responses that emerge when a prototype or intervention encounters its context. Feedback may come from people, materials, organisms, infrastructures or environmental conditions. It becomes research evidence when documented, interpreted and used to reconsider the work.

    Critical briefing:

    Turning research into a precise design question and proposition. A critical brief identifies a context, a position and an opportunity or friction, while questioning the assumptions that determine what is framed as a problem and whose interests it serves.

    Critical technological literacy:

    The ability to understand, use and question technologies in relation to their material, social and political conditions. This includes examining infrastructures, ownership, intellectual property, labour, access and obsolescence, alongside technical operation.

    Declaration of Commitments:

    The living research document initiated by each team at the beginning of a sprint. It records the hypothesis, methods and commitments, then accumulates evidence, iterations, failures and changes of direction across Situate, Explore, Build and Reflect. It provides the documented basis for assessment and, by the end of the sprint, becomes the consolidated record of the entire process, serving as an archive and reference for future work.

    Design biographies:

    The collection of things a designer has made and the traces they leave in the world. These biographies connect a design practice to the relationships and consequences that develop around its work. Wakkary, R. (2021). Things We Could Design. MIT Press.

    Design intervention:

    An action that introduces a prototype, practice or proposition into a real situation to investigate and alter existing relationships. Interventions may be physical, digital, performative or organisational, and involve both human and more-than-human participants.

    Design space:

    A physical or digital collection of experiments, references, projects, objects and materials arranged to make relationships visible. It supports the development of a design inquiry by bringing previous work, emerging questions and possible directions into conversation.

    Digital fabrication:

    Making physical artefacts through processes guided by digital design data, such as CNC machining, laser cutting and 3D printing. In MDEF, these processes are used alongside manual and material techniques to support experimentation, construction and iteration.

    Documentation:

    The ongoing recording and interpretation of a design process through text, images, drawings, data, code or other media. Documentation makes decisions, contributions, failures and findings traceable, supporting reflection, assessment and the sharing of knowledge.

    Drivers:

    Wider social, political, economic, technological or ecological forces that contribute to change. Drivers help explain the conditions in which weak signals emerge and the pressures shaping possible futures.

    Ecosystemic relations:

    Interdependencies between bodies, communities, organisms, materials, technologies and environments. An ecosystemic approach considers how a design participates in these relations, including uneven distributions of resources, care, power and consequences.

    Embodied research:

    Inquiry conducted through bodily participation, sensation, movement and interaction. The body becomes a means of encountering and testing a situation, while its experiences are interpreted in relation to cultural norms, material conditions and the researcher's position.

    Embodied technologies:

    Technologies that operate through and with bodies, shaping sensing, movement, perception and interaction. In MDEF, bodily data and wearable systems are examined as cultural and political constructions as well as technical arrangements.

    Emergent futures:

    Futures taking shape through changes and tensions already present in everyday life. MDEF approaches them through situated inquiry and intervention, asking how existing practices might be redirected and who can participate in shaping what follows.

    Emergent practice:

    A developing way of working that takes form through repeated inquiry, making, reflection and collaboration. In MDEF, the hybrid profile developed in the first year can become the basis for a sustained, self-directed project and practice in the second year.

    Emergent technologies:

    Technologies whose capabilities, uses and social roles are still developing. MDEF investigates them through practical experimentation and critical inquiry, examining the narratives, interests, infrastructures and dependencies that shape their adoption.

    First, second and third person perspectives:

    Different ways of positioning the researcher in relation to a socio-technical system. A 1st person perspective involves researching through one's own participation and experience. A 2nd person perspective involves working with others through dialogue and shared activities. A 3rd person perspective involves observing and analysing a situation from a more external position. None of these perspectives is inherently neutral.

    Full-scale prototyping:

    Making and testing at 1:1 scale so that a prototype can encounter actual bodies, materials, loads and spatial conditions. Full scale allows questions of use, assembly and behaviour to be investigated directly, while still leaving room for revision.

    Futures scouting:

    Researching the present through emerging indicators, existing practices and historical experience to identify possible directions of change. Scouting supports the development of questions and scenarios without treating any single future as inevitable.

    Hybrid profile:

    The evolving combination of questions, skills, methods, interests and positions that shapes a student's practice. A hybrid profile develops through connections across disciplines and experiences, and is reviewed through mentoring and reflection throughout MDEF.

    Infrastructures:

    Material, technical, institutional and social arrangements that enable everyday activities. Roads, data centres, energy systems, protocols and maintenance practices shape what can happen, who can participate and where labour and environmental consequences are distributed.

    Iteration:

    Revising a prototype, method or proposition in response to evidence from a previous test. Iteration requires an identifiable connection between what was observed and what changed, making repeated attempts part of a cumulative inquiry.

    Material experimentation:

    Investigating materials through making and testing to understand their properties, behaviours and possible uses. Decisions emerge from encounters with material responses, including deformation, resistance and failure, rather than from a predetermined form alone.

    Materialising morality:

    The idea that technologies give material form to ethical choices by enabling, encouraging or constraining particular actions. Design therefore involves responsibility for how artefacts mediate conduct. Verbeek, P.-P. (2006). Materializing Morality: Design Ethics and Technological Mediation. Science, Technology, & Human Values, 31(3), 361–380.

    More-than-human relations:

    Relationships in which human life and action are understood as intertwined with other organisms, materials, technologies and environments. This perspective asks how design affects these interdependencies and whose needs or forms of existence are recognised or excluded.

    Networks:

    Relationships connecting people, collectives, organisms, institutions and technologies. Understanding a network involves examining the quality of these relationships, their dependencies and inequalities, and the possibilities they create for collective action.

    New normals:

    Previously unfamiliar situations or practices that become accepted as ordinary. Studying new normals means questioning how they emerge, whose interests they serve and which alternatives become harder to imagine.

    Open content:

    Knowledge and documentation shared under explicit terms that permit reuse and adaptation. In MDEF, prototype documentation includes materials, processes, iterations and failures so others can learn from the work. Access to a file and permission to reuse it are distinct considerations.

    Performative methods:

    Research methods that investigate a question by staging or enacting a situation through bodies, actions, rituals or interactions. In MDEF, a performance can activate a prototype and produce evidence about perception, behaviour, social norms and collective relations.

    Positionality:

    The social, cultural, institutional and personal position from which a researcher acts and interprets a situation. Recognising positionality involves examining how one's experiences, privileges, dependencies and assumptions affect access, relationships and knowledge.

    Prototype:

    A provisional artefact, system or enacted proposition made to investigate a question. In MDEF, prototypes generate knowledge through use, testing and interaction with a context. Their value includes what they reveal, unsettle or fail to do.

    Radical contextuality:

    A commitment to engaging with the specific histories, tensions, actors and contingencies of a situation. In MDEF assessment, this means allowing context to shape the design question, methods and intervention, rather than serving only as a setting for a predetermined idea.

    Reflective practitioner:

    A practitioner who examines and adjusts their understanding while acting, and revisits an experience afterwards to inform subsequent work. Reflection connects decisions, observations and consequences within an ongoing practice. Schön, D. A. (1983). The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action. Basic Books.

    Research-through-design:

    Generating knowledge through designing, making and testing. Prototypes and interventions become instruments of inquiry when their development, encounters and consequences are documented and critically interpreted in relation to a research question.

    Self-reflexivity:

    Critically examining how one's subjectivity, assumptions and involvement influence a situation and its interpretation. Self-reflexivity considers the researcher's role in producing knowledge, including what their methods make visible or leave out.

    Shared responsibility:

    The understanding that students, faculty, directors and coordinators all contribute to the conditions for learning. In MDEF, this involves active participation, preparation, dialogue and mutual accountability, alongside the programme's responsibility to provide guidance and support.

    Situated practices:

    Practices grounded in particular places, histories, relationships and material conditions. Their knowledge is partial and accountable to a position, rather than universally applicable or independent of context. See Haraway, D. (1988), Situated Knowledges.

    Socio-technical systems:

    Interdependent arrangements of technologies, people, institutions, practices and values. Their operation cannot be understood through technical components alone, because social relations and technical decisions continually shape one another.

    State of the art:

    A critical account of relevant research, theories, practices and precedents. In MDEF, it helps situate a project within existing work and contemporary debates, clarifying what can be learned, questioned or developed rather than simply collecting references.

    Technological mediation:

    The ways technologies shape perception, action and relationships. A technology influences what can be sensed, measured or done, rather than simply transmitting an intention unchanged. Designing involves examining these effects and their ethical implications. See Verbeek, P.-P. (2006), Materializing Morality.

    Technological solutionism:

    Treating complex social, political or ecological questions primarily as problems to be solved through technology. MDEF challenges this approach by investigating how problems are framed, whose interests a proposed solution serves and what forms of knowledge or action it excludes.

    Vernacular knowledge:

    Knowledge developed and transmitted through everyday practices, local conditions and collective experience. Craft, repair and construction techniques can carry sophisticated material and social understanding. Engaging with this knowledge requires recognising its practitioners, histories and conditions of use.

    Ways of drifting:

    Finding alternative opportunities and directions for a design practice through feeling, sensing, embodying and making. Drifting allows encounters and experiments to redirect an inquiry without assuming its destination in advance.

    Weak signals:

    Early, partial indicators of change that may reveal emerging practices, tensions or possibilities. Their significance depends on context and interpretation; they offer starting points for inquiry rather than reliable predictions of a future event.

    Wearables:

    Artefacts or systems worn on the body that mediate sensing, action or interaction. In MDEF's second sprint, wearables combine bodily inputs, computation and outputs to investigate how technologies participate in constructing bodily experience and collective situations.
